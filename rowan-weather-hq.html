📍 Kansas City, MO — Where Every Kind of Weather Happens!
🌤️ Types of Weather

Tap any card to learn more about that type of weather! ☁️

☀️
Sunny / Clear
No clouds blocking sunlight. Great for playing outside!
Sunny weather happens when high pressure systems push away clouds. The sun heats the ground, which warms the air above it. In Kansas City, summer sunny days can hit 100°F! The sun is 93 million miles away, but its energy still heats our whole planet. ☀️
🌧️
Rain
Water droplets fall from clouds when they get too heavy.
Rain forms when tiny water droplets in clouds combine and grow until they're too heavy to float. A raindrop falls at about 14 mph. The smell of rain on dry ground is called petrichor — caused by a chemical called geosmin released from the soil. Kansas City gets about 38 inches of rain per year! 🌧️
❄️
Snow
Ice crystals fall from clouds — every snowflake is unique!
Snow forms when water vapor freezes directly into ice crystals in very cold clouds (below 32°F). Each snowflake is a unique hexagonal (6-sided) crystal. No two are exactly alike — scientists have proven it! Kansas City averages about 15 inches of snow per year, mostly January through March. ❄️
⛈️
Thunderstorm
Lightning, thunder, heavy rain, and strong winds all at once!
Thunderstorms need three things: moisture, rising warm air, and something to lift that air (like a cold front). Lightning is a giant spark of electricity — hotter than the surface of the sun! Thunder is the sound of air rapidly expanding from that heat. Light travels faster than sound, so you see lightning before you hear thunder. Count the seconds between flash and thunder and divide by 5 for miles away! ⚡
🌨️
Sleet
Rain that refreezes into tiny ice pellets before hitting the ground.
Sleet forms when rain falls through a layer of freezing air near the ground and turns back into ice before landing. It's different from freezing rain, which stays liquid and freezes ON the ground (creating dangerous ice). Sleet makes a tapping sound on windows and can make roads very slippery. 🌨️
🌫️
Fog
A cloud that forms right at ground level — like walking through a cloud!
Fog is essentially a low cloud touching the ground. It forms when moist air near the surface cools enough for water vapor to condense into tiny droplets. Dense fog can reduce visibility to near zero! Fog is measured in visibility — "pea soup fog" means you can't see more than 1/4 mile. Kansas City often gets morning fog along the Missouri River. 🌫️
🌩️
Hail
Balls of ice that fall from powerful thunderstorms — sometimes huge!
Hail forms inside powerful thunderstorms with strong updrafts. A hailstone starts as a small ice pellet that gets carried up and down repeatedly inside the storm — each trip adds another layer of ice. You can count the rings of a hailstone like tree rings! The largest hailstone ever found in the US fell in Vivian, South Dakota in 2010 — it was 8 inches wide! 🧊
💨
Wind
Moving air caused by differences in air pressure across the atmosphere.
Wind is simply air moving from areas of high pressure to areas of low pressure. The bigger the difference, the stronger the wind! Wind is measured using the Beaufort Scale (0–12). Wind names depend on the direction they come FROM: a north wind blows FROM the north. The jet stream is a river of fast-moving air miles above us that steers our weather systems! 💨
🌈
Rainbow
Sunlight split into colors by raindrops — pure magic science!
Rainbows form when sunlight enters a raindrop, bounces off the back, and exits — splitting into all its colors because each color bends at a slightly different angle. You always see a rainbow opposite the sun (sun behind you, rain in front). Double rainbows happen when light bounces TWICE inside the drop — the second arc has the colors reversed! 🌈
🌡️
Heat Wave
Dangerously hot weather that lasts several days in a row.
A heat wave is declared when temperatures are significantly above normal for at least 3 days in a row. Heat waves actually kill more people in the US each year than any other weather event — more than hurricanes, tornadoes, or floods! The "heat index" (feels-like temperature) combines heat and humidity. Kansas City hit a heat index of 115°F in the summer of 2012! 🥵
🌀
Hurricane
A massive rotating tropical storm with winds over 74 mph.
Hurricanes form over warm ocean water (at least 79°F) when warm moist air rises and cold air rushes in to replace it, creating a giant spinning storm. They can be 300–500 miles wide! The center (called the "eye") is eerily calm and clear. By the time they reach Kansas City they've weakened, but can still bring heavy rain and strong winds. 🌀
🌁
Blizzard
A severe snowstorm with heavy snow, strong winds, and very low visibility.
A blizzard officially requires winds of at least 35 mph, heavy snow, and visibility less than 1/4 mile — all for at least 3 hours. The wind drives the snow sideways, making it feel much colder (wind chill). The Blizzard of 1978 is still one of the most famous in US history. Kansas City has had some memorable blizzards, especially in 1979 and 2011! 🌁

In real meteorology, weather types often combine or cause each other! Sleet really does form from rain + freezing air. A thundersnow really is a snowstorm + thunderstorm. Scientists who study weather (meteorologists) have to think about how all these ingredients interact. You're thinking like a real scientist! 🔬

📐 The Magic Formulas

To convert Fahrenheit → Celsius: subtract 32, then multiply by 5/9
Example: 100°F − 32 = 68 × 5/9 = 37.8°C

To convert Celsius → Fahrenheit: multiply by 9/5, then add 32
Example: 30°C × 9/5 = 54 + 32 = 86°F

72°F / 22°C — Perfect comfortable room temperature
32°F / 0°C — Water freezes / snow can fall

🌬️ Wind Chill & Heat Index

Temperature isn't the whole story! Wind chill is how cold it feels when wind blows — at 20°F with 30 mph wind, it feels like -2°F! The heat index (feels-like temperature) adds humidity — at 95°F with 80% humidity, it feels like 133°F! That's why meteorologists always say "feels like..." on hot or cold days.

🔬 How Weather Works
🌍 The Big Picture

All weather on Earth is powered by just two things: energy from the sun and water moving through the atmosphere. The sun heats the Earth unevenly — the equator gets more heat than the poles — and all our weather is basically the atmosphere trying to balance that out by moving warm and cold air around!

☁️ The Water Cycle
1

Evaporation

The sun heats water in oceans, lakes, and rivers, turning it from liquid into invisible water vapor that rises into the air. Every day, the sun evaporates trillions of gallons of water! Kansas City's Missouri River contributes lots of moisture to local weather.

2

Condensation

As water vapor rises, it cools down. Cool air can't hold as much moisture as warm air, so the vapor condenses back into tiny liquid water droplets around tiny particles like dust or pollen. These droplets form clouds!

3

Precipitation

When cloud droplets combine and grow too heavy to float, they fall as precipitation — rain, snow, sleet, or hail depending on the temperature. Rain is liquid, snow is ice crystals, sleet is refrozen rain, hail is layered ice from thunderstorms.

4

Collection & Runoff

Precipitation collects in oceans, lakes, rivers, and groundwater. Some soaks into the ground, some flows into streams and rivers (runoff), and some is taken up by plants and trees. Then the whole cycle starts over again!

5

Transpiration

Plants release water vapor through their leaves — this is called transpiration. Forests act like giant humidifiers! Combined with evaporation, this is called evapotranspiration. A large oak tree can release 40,000 gallons of water vapor into the air every year. 🌳

🌡️ Air Pressure & Fronts

High pressure = air sinking = clear, calm, sunny weather. Low pressure = air rising = clouds, rain, and storms. A cold front is where cold air pushes under warm air — it brings storms and then clears up. A warm front is where warm air slides over cold air — it brings slow, steady rain. When you see a weather map with "H" and "L" symbols — now you know what they mean! 🗺️

📡 How Meteorologists Predict Weather

Weather forecasting uses Doppler radar (detects rain, snow, and wind), weather satellites (see clouds from space), weather balloons (measure conditions miles up), weather stations (ground readings everywhere), and supercomputers that run complex models. Modern 5-day forecasts are about as accurate as 1-day forecasts were in the 1980s — technology has gotten incredible!

🏆 World Weather Records
🔥
Hottest

Hottest Temperature Ever Recorded — Furnace Creek, Death Valley, CA (1913)

134°F (56.7°C) on July 10, 1913. Death Valley is the hottest, driest, lowest place in North America. Ground temperatures can reach 200°F — hot enough to fry an egg! Daytime temperatures above 120°F are common in summer.

🥶
Coldest

Coldest Temperature Ever Recorded — Vostok Station, Antarctica (1983)

-128.6°F (-89.2°C) on July 21, 1983. That's so cold that exposed skin would freeze in seconds and fuel lines solidify. Scientists at Vostok can't fly in or out for months during winter. The coldest recorded temperature in the US was -80°F in Alaska.

💨
Windiest

Highest Wind Speed Ever — Barrow Island, Australia (1996)

253 mph (408 km/h) during Tropical Cyclone Olivia. For comparison, an EF5 tornado needs only 200+ mph winds! The windiest city in the US is Dodge City, Kansas — which is about 5 hours west of Kansas City.

🌧️
Rainiest

Wettest Place on Earth — Mawsynram, India

Averages 467 inches (11,871 mm) of rain per year — that's nearly 39 FEET of rain! Clouds from the Bay of Bengal slam into the Khasi Hills and are forced upward, dumping unbelievable amounts of rain. Kansas City gets about 38 inches per year — Mawsynram gets 12 times more!

☀️
Sunniest

Driest Place on Earth — Atacama Desert, Chile

Some parts of the Atacama Desert haven't seen rain in recorded history. It's so dry that NASA uses it to test Mars rovers! The desert exists because cold ocean currents on one side and the Andes mountains on the other prevent moisture from ever reaching it.

🧊
Snowiest

Most Snow in One Season — Mount Baker, Washington (1998–99)

1,140 inches (95 feet!) of snow in a single season. That's almost 10 stories of snow. The snowiest city in the US is Flagstaff, Arizona — which gets about 100 inches per year. Heavy lake-effect snowstorms near Lake Erie and Lake Ontario can dump 5–6 feet overnight!

⚡
Stormiest

Most Lightning Strikes — Lake Maracaibo, Venezuela

Lightning flashes here up to 280 nights per year for an average of 9 hours per night — sometimes 28 lightning bolts per minute! It's called "Catatumbo Lightning" and has been used as a navigational landmark for centuries. Locals call it the "Lighthouse of Maracaibo."

🌊
Storm Surge

Deadliest Weather Event in US History — Galveston Hurricane (1900)

A Category 4 hurricane slammed Galveston, Texas with a storm surge of 15 feet, killing between 6,000–12,000 people. There was no warning system at the time. This disaster led to the creation of the National Hurricane Center. After the storm, the city built a 17-foot seawall.

🌨️
Weirdest

Largest Hailstone Ever — Vivian, South Dakota (2010)

A hailstone 8 inches wide and 1.9 pounds fell during a severe thunderstorm. That's bigger than a softball — closer to the size of a small bowling ball! Hail of this size falls at over 100 mph and can punch through car roofs and shatter windows instantly.

🌡️
Weirdest

Biggest Temperature Swing in 24 Hours — Browning, Montana (1916)

Temperature dropped 100°F in a single day — from 44°F to -56°F! This was caused by an Arctic air mass rapidly replacing warm air. Kansas City has had swings of 50–60°F in a single day during spring cold fronts. Wild weather is part of living in the middle of the country!

🤯 Mind-Blowing Weather Facts

Tap any card to unlock the hidden bonus details! 👇

⚡
Lightning strikes Earth 100 times every single second
That's 8 million lightning bolts per day — all happening at once around the world.
👆 Tap for the full story
At any given moment, there are about 2,000 thunderstorms happening on Earth simultaneously. Each bolt is about 5 miles long, 1 inch wide, hotter than the sun's surface (54,000°F!), and happens in about 1/1000th of a second. Over your lifetime, you have about a 1 in 15,000 chance of being struck — but the odds go way up if you're outside in a storm! ⚡
🌧️
A single cloud can weigh over a million pounds
Clouds look light and fluffy — but they're surprisingly heavy.
👆 Tap for the full story
A typical cumulus cloud contains about 500 metric tons (over 1 million pounds) of water droplets! It floats because the droplets are so tiny and spread out that the density is less than the surrounding air — like how a very large but very light piece of foam floats. A cumulonimbus (thunderstorm) cloud can hold 300,000 tons of water! 🌧️
❄️
No two snowflakes have ever been identical — proven by science
You've heard this before — but the reason WHY is mind-blowing.
👆 Tap for the full story
A snowflake's shape is determined by the exact path it takes through the atmosphere — the precise temperature and humidity at every single instant of its journey. Even tiny differences create different crystal shapes. Mathematicians have calculated the number of possible snowflake structures is so astronomically large (10 to the power of 158!) that duplicate snowflakes are essentially impossible. ❄️
🌈
You can never reach the end of a rainbow — ever
It's not just a saying. There's a real scientific reason why.
👆 Tap for the full story
A rainbow is not an object — it's an optical effect that appears at a specific angle (42°) between your eyes, the sun, and raindrops. As you move toward where you think the rainbow is, the angle changes and the rainbow appears to move away. You're always at the center of your own rainbow's circle — and from an airplane, you can see a rainbow as a complete circle! 🌈
🌡️
Venus is hotter than Mercury — despite being farther from the sun
The atmosphere makes all the difference — and it's a warning for Earth.
👆 Tap for the full story
Mercury has no atmosphere, so heat escapes. Venus has a thick atmosphere of CO2 that traps heat in a runaway greenhouse effect — surface temperatures reach 900°F, hot enough to melt lead! This is why scientists worry about too much CO2 in Earth's atmosphere. Earth's weather system naturally regulates temperature — but it needs balance. 🌍
🌊
The ocean is the world's biggest weather machine
Ocean currents move more water than all Earth's rivers combined.
👆 Tap for the full story
The Gulf Stream — a powerful warm ocean current — carries more water than 300 Amazon Rivers! It flows from the Gulf of Mexico up the US East Coast and across to Europe, keeping places like England much warmer than they should be at that latitude. Without the Gulf Stream, London would have winters like northern Canada! Ocean currents are basically Earth's central heating system. 🌊
☀️
The sun's energy powers every single weather event on Earth
Every storm, every breeze, every rain shower traces back to the sun.
👆 Tap for the full story
The sun sends 173,000 terawatts of energy to Earth — that's 10,000 times more than all of humanity uses. Only 0.023% of this energy drives all Earth's weather, ocean currents, and wind. The rest is reflected or absorbed as heat. A single hurricane releases heat energy equivalent to about 10,000 nuclear bombs per day — all powered by the sun through evaporation! ☀️
🌀
Jupiter's Great Red Spot is a storm bigger than Earth — going for 350+ years
Our weather seems crazy — then you see what other planets deal with.
👆 Tap for the full story
Jupiter's Great Red Spot is a storm that's been raging continuously since at least 1665 — and it's 2–3 times wider than Earth! Saturn has a hexagonal storm at its north pole the size of two Earths. Neptune has winds up to 1,500 mph — the fastest in the solar system. Our weather is wild, but we got lucky with a relatively calm planet! 🌀
📖 Weather Word Bank

Real words used by meteorologists — tap any to expand! 🔬

Meteorologist ▼
A scientist who studies the atmosphere and predicts weather. The word comes from the Greek "meteoron" meaning "thing in the sky." Meteorologists use radar, satellites, weather balloons, and supercomputers to forecast weather. The National Weather Service has offices all across the US — including one in Pleasant Hill, Missouri, right near Kansas City!
Atmosphere ▼
The layer of gases surrounding Earth, held in place by gravity. It's about 300 miles thick, but 99% of it is within 20 miles of the surface. It has 5 layers: the troposphere (where all weather happens), stratosphere, mesosphere, thermosphere, and exosphere. The air we breathe is about 78% nitrogen, 21% oxygen, and 1% other gases including CO2 and water vapor.
Front ▼
The boundary between two different air masses (large bodies of air with similar temperature and humidity). Cold fronts (cold air pushing into warm) bring stormy weather that clears quickly. Warm fronts (warm air sliding over cold) bring prolonged rain. Stationary fronts bring extended periods of the same weather. You'll see fronts on every weather map!
Humidity ▼
The amount of water vapor in the air. Relative humidity is expressed as a percentage — 100% means the air is completely saturated and can't hold more water (which means fog or rain is likely). 50% means the air holds half its maximum possible moisture. High humidity makes hot days feel much worse because sweat can't evaporate efficiently to cool you down. Kansas City summers are famously humid!
Dew Point ▼
The temperature at which air becomes saturated and water vapor starts condensing into liquid. A dew point above 60°F feels humid; above 70°F is oppressive; above 75°F is rare and extremely uncomfortable. Dew point is actually a better measure of "how sticky it feels" than relative humidity. Morning dew on grass forms because the ground cools overnight below the dew point!
Isobar ▼
Lines on a weather map connecting areas of equal atmospheric pressure. Where isobars are close together, the pressure changes rapidly over a short distance — and that means strong winds! Where they're spaced far apart, winds are gentle. The circular pattern of isobars around low pressure systems is what creates the spinning of storms. Isobar literally means "equal weight" in Greek.
Precipitation ▼
Any form of water — liquid or solid — that falls from clouds and reaches the ground. This includes rain, snow, sleet, hail, and freezing rain. It's measured in inches or millimeters — but for snow, you also measure depth. One inch of rain equals about 10 inches of fluffy snow (but much less for heavy, wet snow). Kansas City gets about 38 inches of precipitation per year total.
El Niño / La Niña ▼
Natural climate patterns caused by changes in Pacific Ocean temperatures that affect weather worldwide for months or years. El Niño (warm Pacific) tends to bring wetter winters to the southern US and drier conditions to the north. La Niña (cool Pacific) often brings drier, warmer winters to the southern US and more active tornado seasons to the central US — including Kansas City! Scientists monitor these patterns constantly.
Jet Stream ▼
A fast-moving river of air flowing west-to-east high in the atmosphere (6–9 miles up), reaching speeds of 100–250 mph. The jet stream steers weather systems across the US. When it dips south, it brings cold Arctic air to Kansas City; when it stays north, we get warmer weather. Pilots flying east use the jet stream to save fuel and time — it can cut transatlantic flight times by an hour!
Anemometer ▼
An instrument used to measure wind speed. The most common type has three or four cups that spin in the wind — the faster the wind, the faster the cups spin, and a sensor counts the rotations. Wind speed is measured in miles per hour (mph), kilometers per hour (km/h), or knots (nautical miles per hour). Can you say "anemometer" five times fast? 💨
🏠 Weather Safety
⚠️ Watch vs. Warning — Know the Difference!
👀

WATCH

Conditions are right for dangerous weather to develop. Stay alert, know your shelter, monitor the news. No action yet — but be ready!

🚨

WARNING

Dangerous weather has been seen or confirmed. Take shelter or action RIGHT NOW. Don't wait. Every second matters!

✅ Thunderstorm Safety
  • 🏠 Get inside a sturdy building or hard-topped car — NOT a convertible or shed
  • 📵 Avoid using corded phones, computers plugged in, or plumbing during a storm
  • 🌳 Stay away from tall trees, hilltops, open fields, and water
  • ⚡ If outdoors and lightning is close: crouch low, feet together, cover ears, minimize contact with ground
  • ⏱️ Wait 30 minutes after the last thunder before going back outside
❄️ Winter Storm Safety
  • 🧥 Dress in layers — wool and synthetic fabrics, not cotton (cotton loses warmth when wet)
  • 🚗 Keep a winter kit in your car: blanket, boots, water, snacks, flashlight
  • 🏠 Stay inside during a blizzard — getting lost or stuck in a blizzard is life-threatening
  • 🧤 Watch for frostbite (white/grey skin) and hypothermia (uncontrollable shivering)
  • 🔋 Have a battery-powered or hand-crank radio ready for news and alerts
🌡️ Extreme Heat Safety
  • 💧 Drink water constantly — don't wait until you're thirsty! Aim for 8+ glasses on hot days
  • 🏠 Stay in air-conditioned places during the hottest part of the day (noon–4 PM)
  • 🐕 Never leave pets or people in a car — car temperatures can reach 140°F in minutes!
  • 👕 Wear light-colored, loose-fitting clothing and a hat
  • 🤒 Know signs of heat stroke: hot dry skin, confusion, no sweating — call 911 immediately
❌ Never Do These Things
  • ❌ Touch a downed power line — ever, no matter what
  • ❌ Drive through flooded roads — just 6 inches of water can knock a person down, 2 feet floats a car
  • ❌ Use a gas generator indoors — carbon monoxide is odorless and deadly
  • ❌ Stand under a tree during a lightning storm
  • ❌ Ignore weather alerts — they exist to save your life!
